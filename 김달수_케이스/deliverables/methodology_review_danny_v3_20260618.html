Reviewer Guide

What This Document Is

This is the core analytical methodology for diagnosing individual streamer channels on CHZZK (South Korea's primary live-streaming platform). It covers identity extraction, data collection, measurement correction, cohort construction, multi-axis diagnosis, tradeoff quantification, content channel analysis, and cross-validation.

The methodology has been applied to 2 cases so far (one LoL single-game non-virtual channel, one multi-game virtual channel). Both cases used cohorts of 130–320 channels with peak viewers in the 12–45 range.

Scope of this review: You are reviewing the statistical and analytical methodology — whether the diagnostic framework is sound, whether thresholds are justified, whether biases are accounted for, whether the cohort design is robust. You are NOT reviewing client management, deliverable design, execution coaching, or portfolio infrastructure — those sections are excluded.

What we want from you: Critique the methodology as if reviewing a pre-print's methods section. Flag unjustified assumptions, suggest alternative approaches, identify missing safeguards. If a threshold (e.g., 14h for long-stream detection) looks arbitrary, say so and propose a data-driven alternative.

§1. Identity Extraction

1.1 Input Sources

Three layers are collected in balance:

LayerSourceQuestion
Self-perceptionChannel intro, interview, self-statements"How does the streamer define themselves?"
Content realityRecent broadcast/short-form samples"What are they actually doing?"
Viewer receptionChat/comment/reaction patterns"Why do viewers watch?"

1.2 Extraction Principle — 3-Layer Convergence

The intersection of all three layers is the true identity:

  • Self says it but content doesn't show it → Aspiration (not identity)
  • Content shows it but self is unaware → Latent potential (unconfirmed)
  • Only viewers see it → Misperception (not identity)
  • All three present → Identity confirmed

1.3 One-Line Positioning

The 3-layer convergence is compressed into a single sentence. Format varies by case — "contrast appeal + relationship metaphor" or "intent-reception alignment" — whichever captures the case's essence.

1.4 Entry/Retention Separation

On top of identity, two content functions are made explicit:

  • Entry (inflow) driver: Why a viewer first clicks — surface-level, functional
  • Retention (stay) driver: Why a viewer stays — emotional, relational

Confusing these two derails content strategy.

1.5 Vocabulary Handling

Streamer's own spontaneous vocabulary is treated as an identity marker. In analytical documents, it's preserved in quotes; in anonymized outputs, it's replaced with functional equivalents.

Traps
  • Mistaking broadcast titles/surface expressions for identity without data confirmation.
  • Accepting only self-report. Streamers tend to describe their aspirational self. Cross-validate with viewer reception and content reality.
  • Confirming an integrated interpretation without the streamer's explicit validation.

§2. Data Collection & Integrity Verification

2.1 Channel Data

Collect 1 year of daily statistics. 6 months = do not start (see traps).

Required columns: date, followers (cumulative), broadcast hours, peak viewers, average viewers, chat (6-min average or cumulative), viewership (if available).

2.2 Cohort Population Data

Obtain top 2,000 channel rankings in the subject's category from external statistics services. Minimum columns: channel name, broadcast hours, peak viewers, average viewers, viewership.

2.3 Follower Matching Data

Cohort channels' follower counts may not appear in the population ranking. Separately match follower rankings (top 6,000+).

2.4 Verification Procedure

Check all 5 immediately upon receipt. If any fails, do not proceed.

  1. Period completeness — No missing days? End date is current?
  2. Outliers — Peak < average? Impossible values?
  3. Broadcast hour distribution — Frequency of <0.1h or >24h entries?
  4. Category consistency — Does the subject actually belong to this population's category?
  5. Cross-source match — Same channel's numbers match between own data and population data?

2.5 Collection Provenance Schema

Every collected value must be traceable. Required metadata columns:

run_id, source_name, source_url, request_url, platform, cohort_type, aggregation_window_start, aggregation_window_end, fetched_at, http_status, content_type, raw_path, response_hash, parse_status, missing_reason, boundary_signal, collector_version

  • Without raw_path, structured values are notes, not canonical records.
  • Without missing_reason, blank cells are ambiguous between absence and parse failure.
  • Without aggregation_window, ranking numbers risk cross-period contamination.

2.6 Absence Inventory

"Missing" is not discarded — it's stored as data in four states:

StateMeaningTreatment
presentConfirmed to existPreserve source URL/raw path
absentChecked, actually doesn't existRecord absence source + confidence
unknownCannot determine yetLeave open
not_collectedNot collectedMark as out of collection scope

2.7 Privacy Minimization

Streamer diagnosis is public-channel-level analysis. No individual viewer/donor/account tracking. Chat/comments are anonymized by reaction type and vocabulary, never by individual user.

Traps
  • 6-month trend judgment. 6 months cannot control for seasonality. Always use 1 year.
  • Single-broadcast category assignment. One recent broadcast ≠ category. Check distribution.
  • Population misidentification. "Top 2,000 in category" ≠ "all streamers". No extrapolation.

§3. Measurement Correction — Long-Stream / No-Sign-Off Effects

3.1 Average Viewer Distortion Mechanism

Average viewers = total viewership ÷ broadcast hours. When broadcast hours increase (including low-viewer late-night/dawn hours), the denominator inflates and the average is mechanically depressed. This is a measurement effect, not a demand decrease.

3.2 Long-Stream (No-Sign-Off) Identification Criteria

  • Threshold: Daily broadcast time ≥ 14 hours
  • Habitual classification: 5+ days/year at 14h+ OR par (broadcast hours / avg viewers) > 4

Days and channels meeting these criteria are flagged separately.

3.3 Correction Methods (choose 1 or combine)

MethodCalculationStrengthWeakness
A. Peak ratioCorrected avg = peak × 0.62 (normal-channel median ratio)Simple, long-stream robustVulnerable to single spikes
B. Time-weighted avgΣ(avg×hours) ÷ Σ(hours)Precise, strong for quarterly comparisonAt channel level, denominator skews
C. Exclude long-stream daysSimple average excluding 14h+ daysIntuitiveData loss

Recommended: Channel-level comparison (cohort) → A. Time series → B. Visualization → C.

3.4 Recommended Metric Hierarchy

PriorityMetricUse
1Peak viewersLong-stream robust scale metric
2Chat (6-min avg)Core engagement
3Corrected average viewersRatio calculations
AuxiliaryRaw average viewersNever use alone

3.5 Zero Long-Stream Days Case

When the subject has 0 long-stream days in a year, §3 correction is pass-through. The output is the confirmation itself: "0 long-stream days confirmed, no correction applied." The metric hierarchy still applies; raw average can be used as-is.

Traps
  • Accepting a sudden avg-viewer drop as diagnosis. Avg drop → first check broadcast-hour distribution for that period.
  • Uncritically accepting long-streaming as "strategy". Whether it actually works must be measured.
  • Using only one corrected value. Present both raw and corrected; verify the conclusion holds without correction.
  • Skipping §3 for zero-long-stream cases. §3 is not "skipped" but "pass confirmed". Output must state "0 long-stream days confirmed."

§4. Cohort Construction

4.1 Peer Definition

Peak-based is default. Followers include cumulative/dormant accounts and misrepresent current live scale. Peak is a direct measurement of real-time scale.

  • Peer range: Subject's peak × 0.5 to × 2.0

4.2 Filter Pipeline

StageFilterTypical Size
PopulationTop 2,000 in category2,000
1st passPeak 0.5–2.0× filter~200
2nd passRemove esports teams / tournament / corporate accounts~190
3rd passFollower matching (separate data)~150
FinalRemove clear outliers (dormant, <2 months active)~130

4.3 Boundary Cases — Do Not Exclude by Surface

Boundary CaseCheck MethodDecision
Virtual channelsCompare ratio/loyalty distributions vs non-virtualIf distributions overlap → include; clearly different → separate
Different game (secondary)Category distribution (broadcast days)If primary matches → include
Very small channelsBelow peak floorSeparate pool

4.4 Robustness Verification (Required)

Before finalizing cohort conclusions, verify that the subject's position holds across filter variants:

Additional FilterPurpose
Broadcast hours 10h+ / 20h+ / 30h+ onlyAddress short-broadcast ratio inflation
Single category only (subject's category)Remove cross-game noise
Non-virtual onlyAddress virtual/non-virtual difference
Non-long-stream onlyCheck correction dependency

Subject holds top position across all filters → Robust. Any filter causes significant position shift → Investigate that factor deeply.

4.5 Category Confidence

Categories are not determined from a single recent broadcast. Use 90-day or 1-year category distribution when possible.

ConfidenceCondition
highPrimary category share is dominant; profile/history/ranking align
mediumPrimary visible but mixed; auxiliary cohort needed
lowRecent broadcast, profile, ranking disagree; manual review needed

4.6 Peak Basis Multiplexing

The 0.5–2.0× band uses peak as default, but raw max peak alone is vulnerable to raids/events/one-time spikes. Preserve multiple peak measures:

BasisUse
peak_maxExternal ranking, maximum mobilization
peak_p95Extreme spike mitigation
peak_medianRegular tier
peak_recent_medianCurrent tier
peak_recent_p95Current tier including recent spikes

4.7 Primary / Auxiliary Cohort Operation

Boundary channels are not forced into a single cohort. Primary cohort = main-text conclusions. Auxiliary cohort = robustness verification. If the auxiliary cohort significantly changes conclusions, the report states "conclusion depends on market definition."

Traps
  • Excluding categories/virtual by intuition. Check distributions first, then decide.
  • Population misidentification. Is the population "LoL category" or "all CHZZK"? No extrapolation across boundaries.
  • Peak ceiling bias. If peak 240 ceiling is used, large-channel ratios are lost. Verify conclusion impact.

§5. 6-Stage Diagnostic Analysis

5.1 Descriptive Statistics

Look at the cohort distribution first. Understand the population before locating a single channel.

  • Ratio distribution (histogram, quantiles)
  • Follower distribution
  • Peak distribution
  • Broadcast hour distribution

5.2 Multi-Axis Subject Positioning

At least 3 axes:

AxisMeaning
Ratio (loyalty) rankEfficiency — high engagement per follower?
Peak rankAbsolute scale
Follower rank (ascending)Tier / weight class
Broadcast hour rankActivity intensity

5.3 Homogeneity Check

Is the cohort actually a homogeneous group? Proxy measures for cohesion:

  • par (broadcast hours / avg viewers): ≥4 = long-stream type, check distribution
  • Loyalty (peak / avg viewers): 1.5–3 normal; above = one-time spike pattern

5.4 Key Relationship Quantification

Quantify the relationship between the subject's goal (e.g., 10K followers) and current strength (e.g., ratio) using cohort data:

  • Follower vs ratio correlation (log-log)
  • Median ratio by follower band
  • Regression or quantile estimation

5.5 Correction Robustness

Check §3 correction dependency:

  • Does the conclusion hold with raw metrics? (If not → conclusion depends on correction assumptions)
  • What happens to the cohort when long-stream channels are removed?

5.6 Time Series Trend

Subject's 1-year quarterly trends using normal-day (non-long-stream) data:

  • Quarterly peak viewers
  • Quarterly chat
  • Quarterly follower growth rate
  • Relative speed of the three (which is fastest?)

5.7 Scheduling Effect Analysis

Performance is affected by broadcast time and day-of-week. Where possible, attach scheduling columns to daily data and analyze:

  • Is growth from content change or scheduling change?
  • Weekday/weekend and evening/late-night performance gaps?
  • Do long broadcasts depress average viewers, or do they function as retention assets?

5.8 Spike vs Regular Tier Separation

Metric MovementInterpretation
peak_max only risesEvent/raid/special content possible
peak_median risesRegular-tier growth
avg_viewers risesRetention/scheduling stability possible
chat risesCore engagement strengthening
followers only risesExternal inflow present, but live conversion may be weak

In §5 summaries, do not close with a single "growing/stagnating." Separate: spike growth / regular-tier growth / follower growth / core-reaction growth.

Traps
  • Diagnosing a sudden avg-viewer drop before checking broadcast-hour distribution for that period.
  • Claiming "growth" from peak_max alone when peak_median is flat.

§6. Target Tradeoff Quantification

6.1 Conflict Identification

Is there a conflict between the subject's goal (e.g., 10K followers, avg 100) and current strength metric (e.g., ratio)? Verify with cohort data.

6.2 Achievable Range Estimation

Estimate how far the strength metric drops when the goal is reached:

MethodDescription
A. Cohort band medianApply median of cohort's follower-band values
B. Log-log regression extrapolationExtrapolate from fitted regression

When the two methods diverge significantly, present both as an uncertainty range.

6.3 Recommended Target + Alert Floor

  • Recommended target: "Realistic upper tier" within cohort peers
  • Alert floor: Below this → "Growth is not converting to genuine retention"

6.4 Perpetual-Intent Cases

When the streamer explicitly states no intention to graduate and will continue even if shrinking, the recommended target becomes a waypoint rather than a destination. Two additional questions:

  1. Threshold vs sub-threshold structure: Aim for income threshold? Or build a sustainable sub-threshold structure?
  2. Deadline vs perpetual absorption: Set a deadline for the target? Or absorb it as a waypoint in a perpetual flow?

Perpetual-intent is identified from interview signals: "no graduation plan," "will continue even if smaller," "no deadline."

6.5 Monetization Acceptance: Barriers Over Amounts

For revenue/donation analysis, record whether the streamer can accept/request support rather than exact amounts. Privacy-first: amounts are categorized, not stored precisely. Individual donor identification is never stored.

Traps
  • Assuming fixed-ratio growth. "Maintaining current ratio at 10K" is nearly impossible. Acknowledge tier-up cost.
  • Binary framing. Not "ratio vs scale, pick one" — the real question is "which ratio band to defend."
  • Ignoring perpetual intent. Standard deadline-type targets can misalign with a perpetual-intent streamer.

§7. Content Channel Effect Analysis

7.1 External Content Conversion Measurement

Measure whether the subject's external channels (YouTube shorts, Instagram, Twitter, etc.) convert to main-channel growth.

Core question: Does reach (views) on external content convert to main-channel followers/viewers?

Method:

  1. Identify external content posting dates
  2. Compare main-channel follower growth rate in N days before vs after posting
  3. Difference from baseline growth rate = conversion effect

7.2 Identity Alignment Assessment

Even if conversion happens, verify whether incoming viewers match the streamer's identity. Misaligned inflow doesn't retain.

ClassificationDescription
AlignedContent matches main category
PartialIdentity adapted to trend
MisalignedPure trend-chasing (high reach, no identity connection)

7.3 Recommended Direction

  • Aligned content → Main lever
  • Partial → Supplementary, requires identity integration
  • Misaligned → Non-priority (reach/refresh use only)

7.4 Content Segment Sampling

A broadcast is not one monolithic content piece. Actual appeal and conversion candidates emerge from specific segments within a broadcast. Sample segments with:

content_mode, identity_marker, entry_hook, residue_hook, chat_spike, shorts_candidate

7.5 External Channel Conversion Funnel

Separate reach from conversion. External content may have high views but zero main-channel inflow.

  • Reach metrics: views, likes, comments, shares
  • Conversion metrics: follower_delta, next_stream_peak_delta, next_stream_avg_delta
  • Alignment metrics: identity_fit, CTA presence, main-channel link presence

7.6 Viewer Reception Vocabulary Sampling

Why viewers stay cannot be measured by numbers alone. From public reactions, record reaction types and reception vocabulary (anonymized — no individual tracking).

Traps
  • Judging effect by views alone. Reach ≠ conversion. 50K views with zero main-channel inflow = weak effect.
  • Ignoring external channel as standalone asset. Even without main-channel conversion, the external channel (e.g., YouTube subscribers) may be an asset on its own.

§8. External Cross-Validation (XGPS)

8.1 Validation Target Selection

Submit the most reversible or most important conclusions to external validation. Recommended targets (~3):

  • The conclusion with the largest prior correction
  • The core correction assumption (e.g., long-stream correction)
  • The core strategic conclusion

8.2 Prompt Design — Hide Our Conclusions

The core XGPS principle: prevent the external model from inferring our conclusion.

ForbiddenRecommended
"Is our analysis that this channel is growing correct?""From the attached data, determine whether this channel is growing or stagnating."
"There's a long-stream artifact in this data, confirm it""Evaluate whether the average viewer metric can be used as the primary metric for this data."

Attach raw data only. Remove all our corrections and interpretations.

8.3 Convergence / Divergence Handling

ResultInterpretation
ConvergenceConfidence increase — but if it's common-knowledge convergence, weak validation
DivergenceXGPS's real harvest. Possible blind spot. Re-verify with data.
Convergence with refinementExternal provides sharper framing — accept it

Most common mistake: Reinterpreting divergence to defend our conclusion. Don't. Either settle it with data, or leave it as unresolved-open.

8.4 Same-Species Limitation

  • If the external model is also an LLM, shared biases may exist
  • Statistical-common-knowledge convergence is "common sense reconfirmation," not "independent validation"
  • Real value is in divergence and refinement

8.5 Verification Input Package

IncludeExclude
Raw daily statisticsOur conclusion statements
Final cohort CSVCorrection interpretation statements
Cohort methodology summaryClient sensitive information
Content segment sample (partial)Individually identifiable viewer reactions
Unresolved-open list"Is this conclusion correct?" style leading questions
Traps
  • Defensive reinterpretation of divergence. The impulse to rationalize unfavorable signals grows proportionally to their severity. Be aware.
  • Claiming convergence = "validation complete." Convergence increases confidence but is not validation completion.

Appendix A. Risk Awareness Checklist (Abridged)

A.1 Editor Overreach

  • Did I assert a conclusion without team consensus?
  • Did I designate a separate adjudicator for key judgments?
  • Is quick consensus a signal that no real conflict was generated?

A.2 Self-Defense Bias

  • Did I reinterpret external counter-evidence to defend our conclusion?
  • Did I suppress unfavorable signals?
  • Did I close something prematurely with "that's just [obvious reason]"?
  • Did I assert an integrated interpretation without the subject's explicit confirmation?

A.3 Surface Judgment

  • Did I exclude categories/types without data verification?
  • Did I mistake broadcast titles/expressions for identity?
  • Did I assert a trend from short-term (6-month) data?

A.4 Same-Species Dependency

  • Did I over-interpret XGPS convergence as "independent validation"?
  • Did I challenge the core conclusion with at least one different model?

A.5 Silent Domains

  • Did I distinguish "truly absent" from "we didn't look"?
  • Did I separate blank cells into absent / unknown / not_collected?
  • Did I record the original source of each absence (absence_source)?

These are the specific points where we seek your critique. For each, we value: (a) whether the current approach is defensible, (b) specific alternatives you'd recommend, (c) any biases or failure modes we haven't accounted for.

1 §3 — The 14-hour threshold for long-stream detection: is this cutoff justified, or is it arbitrary? Would you use a data-driven method (e.g., distribution inflection point, mixture model) instead?
Context: The 14h threshold was set empirically from Case 1, where a 24h+ broadcast day caused a visible avg-viewer artifact. The par > 4 secondary criterion was added as a ratio-based backstop.
High  If the threshold is wrong, measurement correction either misses distorted channels or over-corrects normal ones. Every downstream percentile is affected.
2 §3 — The peak ratio method uses 0.62 (normal-channel median ratio) as the correction factor. How robust is a single population median as a correction basis? Should channel-specific or band-specific ratios be used instead?
Context: 0.62 is the median of peak/avg ratio across non-long-stream channels in the cohort. It's applied uniformly to all corrected channels.
Normal  Affects corrected avg-viewer values, but §3.5 robustness check verifies conclusions hold without correction.
3 §4 — The peak 0.5×–2.0× band for peer definition: is this range too wide, too narrow, or should it be adaptive (e.g., based on peak distribution density)?
Context: In practice, this produces ~200 channels from a 2,000 population. The band was chosen for balance between sample size and homogeneity.
High  Cohort definition is foundational. If the band systematically includes non-peers or excludes true peers, all §5 percentiles and §6 projections are biased.
4 §4 — Robustness verification uses 4 filter variants. Are these sufficient? Are there confounders (e.g., streaming schedule, content freshness, platform algorithm changes) that the current variants don't test?
Context: Current 4 variants test broadcast hours, category purity, virtual/non-virtual split, and long-stream inclusion. All applied to the same population.
Normal  More variants increase confidence but also analysis cost. The question is whether 4 is the right cost-confidence tradeoff.
5 §4 — When the subject sits at the boundary between two cohorts (e.g., primary cohort percentile 72%, auxiliary cohort 45%), how should the gap be reported? Our current approach is to note it as a known limitation. Your earlier comment suggested follower-adjusted percentile or regression residual. We agree — which would you prioritize for this specific use case?

High  If we can't explain the gap rigorously, the choice of "which cohort's percentile to trust" becomes a judgment call rather than a data-driven decision.
